Research

Microbes run ecosystems everywhere.

The same microbial principles that govern a hydrothermal vent, a coral reef, or the human gut also govern a lake, a reservoir, or an aquifer. We're broadly interested in how microbial life shapes ecosystems wherever it's found — from the deep ocean to the human gut — and we bring that lens to bear on the water systems closest to home.

Right now, our fieldwork and funding are grounded in three connected areas: aquatic ecosystems, water quality, and groundwater.

01 — Aquatic Ecosystems

How microbial life structures whole ecosystems

Microorganisms form the base of aquatic food webs and drive the biogeochemical cycles — carbon, nitrogen, phosphorus — that determine ecosystem productivity. We study how microbial communities respond to seasonal change, nutrient loading, and disturbance across lakes, rivers, and coastal systems, and how those responses cascade up to affect fish, invertebrates, and water clarity.

Current projects examine nutrient cycling in temperate lakes, food-web responses to warming, and the role of microbial mats in shaping littoral zone productivity.

02 — Groundwater

The hidden microbial engine beneath our feet

Groundwater is often treated as a lifeless resource, but aquifers host active microbial communities that shape the water quality of everything from private wells to public supply. We study how these communities degrade contaminants, cycle nutrients, and respond to the exchange of water between aquifers and the surface systems above them.

Current projects trace microbial contaminant-degradation pathways, characterize groundwater–surface water interactions at near-stream aquifers, and track how land use and climate affect the microbial communities that filter the water we pump out of the ground.

03 — Water Quality

Translating microbial science into water we can trust

Our most applied work connects microbial ecology directly to water quality outcomes that matter to people and ecosystems: harmful algal blooms, nutrient pollution, and contaminant cycling across lakes, reservoirs, and the aquifers that feed them. We collaborate with watershed managers and public health partners to develop microbial indicators that can inform monitoring and management decisions.

This includes long-term monitoring of regional lakes and reservoirs, and developing early-warning approaches for cyanobacterial blooms based on molecular signals.
